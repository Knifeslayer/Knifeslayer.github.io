Draft Complete and Trying Something Different — An Essay on Hoid

Posted: 2026-03-15

After finishing the draft for Toby, The Monster Hunter 2, I found myself in a familiar spot: wanting to do something different, but not so different that I vanished into a completely new novel for the next six months.

Brandon Sanderson has talked before about taking breaks between projects by working on something entirely different. That approach makes a lot of sense. For me, though, I thought an essay might be a better version of that idea — different enough to feel fresh, but short enough that I don’t accidentally wake up five weeks later buried in another 120,000 words to edit.

Part of that idea came from this YouTube video, which made a very good case for why more of us should probably write essays:

I'm begging you to write essays

So, instead of starting Book 3 tomorrow like a responsible and completely normal person, I wrote this.

***

Is Hoid the Most Subtle Authorial Self-Insert in Fantasy?

Most author self-inserts are easy to spot. They are too visible, too knowing, or too obviously protected by the story around them. The reader can feel the author reaching onto the page. That is what makes Hoid so interesting. He does not read like a crude projection of Brandon Sanderson into the Cosmere. He reads like something more controlled and more literary: a character who reflects the function of the author without becoming a literal stand-in. Sanderson has said Hoid is the same recurring person across the Cosmere and that he is important, but he has also pushed back on the idea that Hoid is simply Brandon himself written into the books. That tension is exactly what makes Hoid compelling. He may not be the most obvious authorial figure in fantasy, but he has a strong claim to being the most subtle.

The clearest reason is that Hoid is, above all else, a storyteller. This is not just a fan reading imposed onto the text after the fact. In Warbreaker, Hoid is explicitly used as a storyteller, and Sanderson’s own annotation on the chapter explains why: he needed a way to present history and exposition without making the scene lifeless. Hoid becomes the answer. Sanderson describes wanting someone “more interesting” to deliver that material, and Hoid fills that role perfectly. That is a strikingly authorial function. The author takes history, structure, and hidden meaning and turns them into something engaging for the reader. Hoid does the same thing inside the world itself. He is not merely a man who enjoys stories; he is a figure through whom the world becomes narratable. That makes him feel less like an ordinary character and more like a living embodiment of the author’s craft.

Hoid also has a habit of appearing in or near the plot, even when he does not seem central at first. Sanderson’s official FAQ states that Hoid is the same individual across the Cosmere and that readers should pay attention to him because he is relevant to each of them in some way or another. That recurring presence changes how he functions in the narrative. He is not simply a recurring easter egg. He is always near moments of consequence, always brushing against major events. In structural terms, that is very close to what an author is. An author may not stand in the spotlight as a character, but the author is always present wherever meaning gathers, wherever plot tightens, wherever hidden threads begin to show. Hoid’s repeated proximity to key events gives him that same aura. He feels like the story’s awareness of itself, disguised as a traveler with a sharp tongue.

That subtlety becomes even more impressive when Hoid uses storytelling not only for the reader, but for characters inside the narrative. He does not tell stories as decoration. He tells them to influence, guide, or reshape the people around him. Sanderson’s Warbreaker annotation makes this especially clear: Hoid’s storytelling is a way of conveying necessary truth to Siri in dramatic form. He becomes an interpreter between raw history and human understanding. That is exactly what authors do for their characters and readers alike. They do not simply present facts. They arrange those facts into forms that create insight. Hoid repeatedly serves that role within the Cosmere. He nudges characters toward perspective rather than merely feeding them information. He reframes rather than lectures. He understands that a story can do what a plain answer cannot. In that sense, Hoid is not just a storyteller inside the narrative; he is a symbol of Sanderson’s larger belief that story itself is a tool of revelation.

His age and origin deepen that parallel. Hoid is not just old in the ordinary fantasy sense. Canon material and Words of Brandon place him at Yolen and connect him to the Shattering of Adonalsium, one of the defining foundational events of the Cosmere. Future plans have pointed toward another Cosmere book series, Dragonsteel, which will explore Hoid’s origin more directly and reinforce how closely he is tied to the beginning of the universe’s known history. That matters for this argument because it makes him a centerpiece for the perspective of those historic events. He stands near the origin of the world’s deepest story, much as the author stands at the origin of the world itself. Hoid is not the creator of the Cosmere, of course, but he carries the feeling of someone who has been present since the earliest design. He moves through the world with an almost creator-adjacent awareness, which makes the comparison to an authorial presence feel even stronger.

Another reason Hoid works so well is that he bridges story and meta-story without openly breaking the narrative. Sanderson has spoken often about the Cosmere as a larger connected project, a “story behind the story,” and Hoid is one of the main ways readers begin to sense that larger architecture. He teaches the audience how to read the Cosmere. Once readers understand that Hoid is recurring, every appearance invites a new level of attention. Suddenly, the books are not only individual narratives; they are pieces of a wider design. That is another deeply authorial quality. Hoid does not merely exist in the story. He helps reveal that the story has been arranged. Yet he does this without collapsing into overt metafiction. He does not usually stop the narrative and announce its construction. Instead, he remains within the illusion of the world while hinting at its hidden shape.

This is where comparison becomes useful. There are more obvious authorial figures in literature and fantasy-adjacent fiction. Stephen King famously writes a fictionalized Stephen King into The Dark Tower, making the author’s presence direct and unmistakable. Grant Morrison does something similarly explicit in Animal Man, appearing in the text and confronting the character from the position of creator. Kurt Vonnegut’s work, especially through figures like Kilgore Trout and the metafictional moves in Breakfast of Champions, also pushes openly toward author-as-character territory. These are powerful examples, but they are not subtle. Their brilliance comes from exposure. They invite readers to notice the author stepping onto the stage. Hoid achieves something different. He remains internal to the fantasy world. He does not break the illusion; he strengthens it, even while carrying an unmistakably author-like role. That is what makes him such a persuasive candidate for the most subtle version of this device.

What ultimately makes Hoid so effective is that he reflects Brandon Sanderson’s role rather than his literal personality. Sanderson has said Hoid is not just a simple cameo and is not really “him” in any direct way. That separation matters. If Hoid were too obviously Brandon in costume, the effect would collapse into gimmick. Instead, Hoid remains his own person: secretive, manipulative, amusing, unsettling, at times compassionate, and at times difficult to trust. He has his own motives and his own goals. That independence is precisely why the authorial parallel feels sophisticated rather than indulgent. Hoid works because he is not Sanderson copied into the page. He is a character shaped to echo what an author does: preserve history, guide attention, connect disparate stories, and speak truth through narrative.

So, is Hoid the most subtle authorial self-insert in fantasy? He has a serious claim. He is a storyteller within the world and a storyteller about the world. He appears where narrative significance gathers. He uses stories to shape characters from within the text. He stands near the oldest roots of the Cosmere, like a witness present almost from creation. And unlike more overt author-surrogates, he never fully tears open the wall between author and story. Hoid does not announce Brandon Sanderson’s presence. He suggests it through function, structure, and symbolism. That is what makes him so effective throughout the text.

***

And lastly, a genuine thank you to Brandon Sanderson for putting so much of his writing advice online for free. His lectures and courses helped a lot back in 2019–2020, when COVID hit and I suddenly had a lot more time to get back into writing. They helped make the whole thing feel possible again, and I’m grateful for that.
